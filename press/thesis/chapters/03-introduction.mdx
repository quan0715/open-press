## 1. Introduction

Heat at pedestrian level is experienced through a combination of air temperature, radiant exposure, wind, humidity, activity, and expectation. Street design cannot control all of these conditions, but it can shape whether a walking route offers continuous protection from direct sun.

This demonstration focuses on a narrow question: **within the fictional sample, how does midday shade continuity relate to measured surface temperature and reported pedestrian comfort?** The study treats shade as a spatial sequence rather than a single point measurement.

### 1.1 Research objectives

1. Describe shade coverage across eight hypothetical street segments.
2. Compare surface-temperature readings under higher and lower shade continuity.
3. Examine whether fictional comfort ratings follow the same pattern.
4. Identify design implications without claiming causation.

### 1.2 Scope

The fictional sites represent compact mixed-use streets during a warm-season observation window. The sample excludes night conditions, extreme weather, indoor comfort, and long-term health outcomes.

### 1.3 Thesis structure

The following chapters establish the conceptual context, explain the demonstration method, report invented results, and separate interpretation from evidence. The final appendix states the sample-data boundary explicitly.
